Amp Hours vs Watt Hours: Converting for a 12V Camper Battery

Short answer: watt-hours (Wh) = amp-hours (Ah) × volts. A 100Ah lithium (LiFePO4) battery at 12.8V nominal holds about 1,280Wh. A 100Ah AGM at 12V holds about 1,200Wh. But you can only use part of that, so the usable figure is what matters.

| Battery | Nominal volts | Stored Wh | Usable share (assumed) | Usable Wh | |---|---|---|---|---| | 100Ah lithium | 12.8V | 1,280 | 80% | 1,024 | | 200Ah lithium | 12.8V | 2,560 | 80% | 2,048 | | 100Ah AGM | 12V | 1,200 | 50% | 600 | | 200Ah AGM | 12V | 2,400 | 50% | 1,200 |

The usable shares of 80% (lithium) and 50% (AGM) are common planning figures, not guarantees. Makers differ, so check your battery's datasheet.

Why two units exist

Amp-hours tell you how much current a battery can supply over time, but only at a given voltage. Watt-hours measure energy directly, so they let you compare a fridge, a laptop and a solar panel on one scale. Panels are sold in watts, loads are often listed in watts, and batteries are sold in amp-hours. Converting everything to Wh makes the sizing simple.

The conversions

  • Wh = Ah × V. 100Ah × 12.8V = 1,280Wh.
  • Ah = Wh ÷ V. 770Wh ÷ 12.8V = about 60Ah.
  • Watts = volts × amps. A fridge drawing 4A at 12V is 48W.
  • Wh per day = watts × hours running. A 60W laptop charger for 2 hours is 120Wh.

A small trap: a fridge duty-cycles. A compressor fridge rated at 45W doesn't run all day, so use a measured or published daily figure if you have one.

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

These loads are assumptions for a weekend-to-week camper setup. Swap in your own.

| Load | Calculation | Wh/day | |---|---|---| | Compressor fridge (assumed daily total) | assumed | 600 | | LED lights | 5W × 4h | 20 | | Phone charging | assumed | 20 | | Laptop | 60W × 2h | 120 | | Water pump | 40W × 0.25h | 10 | | Total | | 770 |

Add a 20% margin for losses and cold days: 770 × 1.2 = 924Wh per day.

Battery size

  • Lithium, one day with no sun: 924 ÷ 0.8 = 1,155Wh usable-adjusted. 1,155 ÷ 12.8 = about 90Ah, so a 100Ah battery just covers one day.
  • Lithium, two days with no sun: 1,848 ÷ 0.8 = 2,310Wh. 2,310 ÷ 12.8 = about 180Ah, so a 200Ah battery.
  • AGM, one day: 924 ÷ 0.5 = 1,848Wh. 1,848 ÷ 12 = about 154Ah, so 150 to 200Ah. This is why lithium often looks smaller on paper for the same job.

Solar size

Using the common rule of solar watts = daily Wh ÷ peak sun hours (PSH):

  • At an assumed 4.5 PSH: 924 ÷ 4.5 = about 205W, so 200 to 250W.
  • At an assumed 3 PSH (a dull winter day in the south): 924 ÷ 3 = 308W, so 300W or more.

Peak sun hours vary a lot by place and season. Alice Springs in winter and Hobart in winter are very different, and a flat panel in shade gives you far less. Treat the figures as starting points and add margin if you camp in cloud or tree cover.

Check the charger side

Once you have a battery, check how fast it can be charged. The sum of your DC-DC charger output and your solar current must stay under the battery's maximum charge current, which is in the battery's specifications. Also note that DC-DC output depends on your alternator and the vehicle wiring, not just the charger's label.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a 12V AGM Ah figure to a 12.8V lithium Ah figure as if they were equal. The 6.7% voltage difference is small, but the usable share difference is large.
  • Using Ah for loads and Wh for solar without converting. Pick one unit, and Wh is easiest.
  • Sizing to 100% of the battery. Leave headroom.
  • Forgetting 240V loads. Running an inverter adds losses, and any mains wiring needs a licensed electrician.

Safety note

This is general information only. Cable sizes, fuses and the installation need a licensed auto-electrician, and anything 240V needs a licensed electrician.
