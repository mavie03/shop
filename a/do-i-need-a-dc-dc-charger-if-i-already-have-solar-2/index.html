Do I Need a DC-DC Charger If I Already Have Solar?

Short answer: often yes, if you want reliable power. Solar is free and silent, but it depends on the sun. A DC-DC charger charges your camper battery from the vehicle while you drive, so it covers cloudy days, shaded camps and winter. If you only do sunny weekends in open country with a big battery, solar alone can be enough.

| Your situation | Solar only OK? | Add DC-DC? | |---|---|---| | Weekends, open sites, summer, modest loads | Usually | Optional | | Shaded campsites (bush, caravan parks) | Rarely | Yes | | Winter travel in southern Australia | Often short | Yes | | Moving most days (touring) | Possible | Cheap top-up, worth it | | Staying put for weeks, big loads | Only with large array | Helps as backup |

What each one does

  • Solar makes energy only in daylight, and the amount depends on panel size, tilt, shade, dust, cloud and season.
  • DC-DC charger takes power from the starter battery or alternator and delivers a controlled charge to the second battery. It makes energy whenever the engine is running, regardless of weather.

They are complementary. Solar tops up slowly while you are parked. DC-DC refills quickly while you drive.

Worked example (all numbers illustrative)

Assumptions, which you should replace with your own:

  • Daily use: 600 Wh (fridge, lights, phone charging, water pump).
  • Battery: 100Ah lithium at 12.8V nominal = 1,280 Wh. At 80% usable (a typical figure that varies by maker) = 1,024 Wh.
  • Solar: 200W panel, with 75% overall efficiency (an assumed allowance for losses).
  • Peak sun hours (flat panel, illustrative): 6 in summer, 3 in a poor winter or shaded situation.

Summer: 200 W × 6 h × 0.75 = 900 Wh/day. That beats 600 Wh, so solar alone copes.

Winter or shade: 200 W × 3 h × 0.75 = 450 Wh/day. You are 600 − 450 = 150 Wh/day short.

How long does the battery last with that shortfall? 1,024 ÷ 150 ≈ 6.8 days. That sounds fine, but one fully overcast stretch is worse. With no solar at all, 1,024 ÷ 600 ≈ 1.7 days.

Adding a 25A DC-DC charger: real output is usually below the rated figure, so assume about 20A on average (an assumption). Two hours driving gives 20A × 2 h = 40Ah. At 12.8V that is about 512 Wh. That one drive covers the 150 Wh winter shortfall for about 3.4 days (512 ÷ 150), or most of a no-sun day's 600 Wh.

The takeaway: in good sun the charger is a nice-to-have. In poor sun it is what keeps the fridge cold.

When solar alone is enough

  • You camp in sunny, open spots and rarely in winter.
  • Your loads are small (a fridge plus lights, say).
  • You have a decent battery to ride out a bad day.
  • You are happy to run a generator or drive somewhere if the battery runs low.

When a DC-DC charger earns its place

  • You camp under trees or in caravan parks with shade.
  • You travel in winter, when days are short and the sun is low.
  • You tow or drive most days, so charging is almost free.
  • You use a lithium battery. Lithium needs a proper charge profile, and a plain connection to the vehicle's alternator is not a good idea. Many modern vehicles also have smart alternators, which behave differently, so check your vehicle's setup.

Sizing the charger

Retailers publish rules of thumb that differ. A common guide pairs roughly 25A with 100-150Ah batteries, 40A with 200-300Ah, and 40-60A with 400-600Ah. Other sources express it as a percentage of battery capacity (somewhere between about 10% and 30%). Treat these as vendor rules, not specifications. Check what your battery maker allows, and how much your alternator can spare after the vehicle's own needs.

Common sizes in Australia are 20A, 25A, 40A and 50A.

Things people miss

  • Charging time is a minimum. Charging slows as the battery nears full, so real times are longer.
  • Short drives give little. A 20-minute trip to the shops adds very little energy.
  • Cables matter. Long runs from the front of the vehicle to a trailer lose voltage, so cable size and fuse choice are important. That is a job for a licensed auto-electrician. Anything involving 240V needs a licensed electrician.
  • Solar numbers are averages. Cloud, dust, shade and tilt change real output a lot.

A quick way to decide

  • Add up your daily watt-hours.
  • Work out your solar output in your worst likely month.
  • If solar covers your use with margin, you may skip the DC-DC charger. If it falls short, or you want a safety net, add one sized to your battery.
