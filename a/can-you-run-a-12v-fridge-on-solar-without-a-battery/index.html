Can You Run a 12V Fridge on Solar Without a Battery?

Short answer: technically sometimes, practically no. A fridge wired straight to a solar panel only runs while the sun is shining, can be damaged by the panel's voltage, and leaves your food warming at night and under cloud. A small battery plus a solar controller is the minimum sensible setup, and it doesn't have to be big.

Why a fridge needs a battery

A 12V compressor fridge doesn't draw power evenly. It cycles on and off around the clock, and it works hardest on hot afternoons and evenings, when solar is fading. A panel produces power only in daylight, and it swings with cloud, dust, shade and angle.

| Problem with panel-direct | What happens | |---|---| | No power at night | The fridge relies on insulation alone for roughly half the day | | Cloud or shade | Output drops and the compressor may stall or restart repeatedly | | Panel voltage | A "12V" panel can have an open-circuit voltage well above 12V, which many fridges aren't designed to accept | | No controller | Nothing manages the panel's voltage or protects the fridge |

Check your fridge maker's manual for its accepted input voltage range. Don't connect a panel straight to any appliance unless the maker says it is designed for that.

What the smallest sensible setup looks like

You need three things: a battery to carry the fridge through the night, a solar charge controller between panel and battery, and a panel big enough to refill the battery each day.

Worked example (illustrative assumptions)

These are typical values, not specs. They vary by fridge, ambient temperature and how often you open the lid.

  • Fridge draws about 40W while running and runs about half the time: 40W × 24h × 0.5 = 480Wh per day.
  • Assume half the running happens when there's no sun: about 240Wh has to come from the battery.
  • Assume 4 peak sun hours a day (a cautious figure for a flat panel in a darker month; your location and season will differ).
  • Assume 75% overall system efficiency after controller, wiring and dust losses.

Battery for the overnight share (240Wh):

  • Lithium (LiFePO4), 12.8V nominal, 80% usable: 240 ÷ 12.8 = 18.75Ah, then ÷ 0.8 = about 23Ah.
  • AGM, 12V nominal, 50% usable: 240 ÷ 12 = 20Ah, then ÷ 0.5 = 40Ah.

Usable depth of discharge varies by maker, so check the datasheet. In practice most people buy bigger than this so the battery isn't cycled hard and can ride through a cloudy day.

Solar to refill a full day (480Wh):

  • 480 ÷ 4 sun hours = 120W, then ÷ 0.75 = 160W of panel.

So on these assumptions, roughly a 160W panel (in practice, a 150–200W panel size), a controller, and a 40Ah-ish battery keeps a fridge going. Change the sun hours and the answer moves a lot: at 2 sun hours (a dull winter stretch), the same maths gives 320W.

Don't forget the cold-weather and cloud limits

  • Several cloudy days in a row will flatten a small battery. Add capacity, or plan to charge from the vehicle while driving.
  • If you choose lithium, charging is limited in cold conditions. Many lithium batteries shouldn't be charged near or below freezing unless they have built-in protection or heating. Check the maker's spec.
  • Some controllers and chargers need a minimum battery or input voltage to start up. Many solar controllers won't operate properly without a battery connected, so read the manual.

Is there a cheaper alternative?

If your fridge runs only on mains or you use an icebox, solar is overkill. But a compressor fridge is the most common big load in a camper, and it's the one that sets your battery and solar size. Work out its real daily watt-hours first, then size everything else around it.

Another option is topping up from the vehicle with a DC-DC charger while driving. That helps on travel days but not at a long camp, so most setups combine both. How much it delivers also depends on your alternator and vehicle wiring.

Safety

This is general information, not electrical design advice. Cable sizes, fuses and connections matter for fire safety and aren't covered here. Have a licensed auto-electrician specify and check the wiring, and use a licensed electrician for anything 240V or mains.
