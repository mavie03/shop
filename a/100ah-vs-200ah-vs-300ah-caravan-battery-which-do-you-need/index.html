100Ah vs 200Ah vs 300Ah Caravan Battery: Which Do You Need?

Short answer: pick the battery by how many days you want to run without charging, then size solar and DC-DC to refill it. In our worked example (about 780 Wh a day including a margin), a 100Ah lithium battery lasts about 1.3 days, 200Ah about 2.6 days and 300Ah about 3.9 days. Light users can be happy with 100Ah. Fridge-plus-gadgets campers usually land on 200Ah. 300Ah suits long stays off-grid or heavy use.

Quick comparison (illustrative)

Assumptions, all estimates you should replace with your own: 12.8V nominal lithium (LiFePO4), 80% usable (this varies by maker, so check your battery's datasheet), a 20% margin already included in the daily figure, and "days" meaning 100% down to 20% with no charging at all.

| Battery | Stored energy | Usable (80%) | 500 Wh/day | 780 Wh/day | 1,800 Wh/day | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | 100Ah | 1,280 Wh | 1,024 Wh | ~2.0 days | ~1.3 days | ~0.6 days | | 200Ah | 2,560 Wh | 2,048 Wh | ~4.1 days | ~2.6 days | ~1.1 days | | 300Ah | 3,840 Wh | 3,072 Wh | ~6.1 days | ~3.9 days | ~1.7 days |

Maths: stored Wh = Ah × 12.8. Usable = stored × 0.8. Days = usable ÷ daily Wh (for example 2,048 ÷ 780 = 2.63).

Step 1: Add up your daily watt-hours

Watt-hours (Wh) = watts × hours used. The fridge is normally the biggest item because the compressor cycles on and off, so use its average daily figure, not its running watts. Here is a made-up but plausible camper-trailer load:

  • 12V fridge: 450 Wh/day (estimate; it climbs in hot weather)
  • LED lights: 30 Wh
  • Water pump: 20 Wh
  • Phones, USB, small fan: 100 Wh
  • Other (router, heater fan, etc.): 50 Wh

Total 650 Wh. Add a 20% margin: 650 × 1.2 = 780 Wh/day. That's the middle column above. Your own numbers will differ, so measure or read the plate ratings of what you own.

Step 2: Choose by days of autonomy, not by habit

Autonomy is the number of days the battery carries you with no sun and no driving. Ask:

  • How long are your stays? Moving every day, with a DC-DC charger topping up while you drive, means you rarely need more than a day or so in reserve.
  • How much shade or bad weather will you accept? Three overcast days in Tasmania in winter is a very different problem to a week of clear sky near Alice Springs.
  • What's your heaviest load? An inverter running a TV, coffee machine or laptop can push you to the 1,800 Wh column fast. (Anything 240V or mains wiring needs a licensed electrician.)

Rough reading of the table: 100Ah suits weekend trips and light loads. 200Ah is the sensible middle for a fridge, lights and charging gadgets. 300Ah is for multi-day stays without moving, or bigger loads. Some retailers also describe 200Ah as a common caravan size; treat that as a vendor view, not a rule.

Step 3: Size solar to refill what you use

Solar watts needed ≈ daily Wh ÷ (peak sun hours × system efficiency). We assume 4 peak sun hours (a winter-ish figure for many southern areas; northern Australia is often higher) and 75% efficiency to allow for heat, dust, wiring and controller losses. Both are assumptions.

| Daily use | Solar needed (4 PSH, 75%) | |---|---| | 500 Wh | ~167 W | | 780 Wh | 260 W | | 1,800 Wh | 600 W |

Maths: 780 ÷ (4 × 0.75) = 780 ÷ 3 = 260 W. So the 780 Wh camper wants roughly 260 W, which in practice means a 300 W setup. A bigger battery doesn't need more solar to run, but it takes longer to refill after a deep draw, so 300Ah banks often want more panels.

Controller check: a commonly used MPPT sizing rule (panel watts ÷ battery volts × 1.25) gives the minimum controller amps. For 300 W at 12V: 300 ÷ 12 × 1.25 = 31.25, so look at a 40A-class controller. Confirm against the controller maker's panel-voltage limits.

Step 4: DC-DC charger and the battery's charge limit

A DC-DC charger lets the vehicle alternator charge the camper battery while you drive. Retailers often suggest roughly 25A for 100–150Ah and around 40A for 200–300Ah. That's a vendor rule of thumb, not a standard.

Two checks people miss:

  • Add the charge sources. DC-DC amps plus solar amps must stay under the battery's maximum charge current in its BMS (battery management system) specification. For example, 40A DC-DC plus about 25A of solar is 65A. A small 100Ah battery rated for less than that would be overdriven, so read your battery's spec sheet.
  • Real output depends on your vehicle. Alternator output, wiring and voltage drop all limit what the charger delivers. The input side draws more current than the output, so cabling should be sized for the input. Cable sizes and fuses are a job for a licensed auto-electrician.

Step 5: Local conditions

  • Cold: lithium batteries usually restrict or stop charging near freezing. If you camp in Snowy Mountains or Tasmanian winters, look for a battery with low-temperature protection or heating.
  • Input voltage: some DC-DC chargers need a minimum input voltage, which matters with smart alternators in newer 4WDs. Check the charger's spec for your vehicle.
  • Space and weight: a 300Ah lithium battery is heavier and costs more. Don't buy capacity you will never use, as it adds to your tow ball weight.

Common mistakes

  • Buying by Ah alone and ignoring that AGM and lithium have different usable capacity (AGM is often treated as about 50% usable).
  • Using the fridge's maximum wattage instead of its daily average.
  • Sizing for a summer sun figure and then camping in winter.
  • Forgetting the margin for cloud, dust and shade.
