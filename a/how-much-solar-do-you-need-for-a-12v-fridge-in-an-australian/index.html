How Much Solar Do You Need for a 12V Fridge in an Australian Winter?

Summer solar is easy. Winter is where setups fall short: shorter days, a lower sun and more cloud, while the fridge still runs. This guide shows how to work out the panel size you need for the darkest part of your trip, with every step and unit spelled out so you can swap in your own numbers.

The short answer

For a typical camp load of about 550 Wh a day (a 12V fridge plus lights, phone charging and a small pump), you need roughly:

  • 160 W of panel with about 5 peak sun hours
  • 200 W with about 4
  • 260 W with about 3
  • 400 W with about 2

Those sun figures are illustrative, not forecasts. Your real number depends on where you are, the month, tilt, shade, dust and cloud. The rest of this article shows where the figures come from.

Step 1: Add up daily watt-hours (Wh)

Watt-hours = watts × hours used per day. For the fridge, use a measured or manufacturer's average daily figure rather than the label's peak wattage, because the compressor cycles on and off.

Example load (all assumptions, replace with yours):

| Item | Calculation | Wh/day | |---|---|---| | 12V fridge (assumed 40 Ah/day at 12 V) | 40 × 12 | 480 | | LED lights | 3 W × 5 h | 15 | | Phone/USB charging | assumed | 40 | | Water pump | 40 W × 0.2 h (12 min) | 8 | | Total | | 543 |

We'll round to about 545 Wh/day. If you have a laptop, a drone battery charger or a second fridge, add them. Hot weather also raises fridge use, so be generous.

Step 2: Understand peak sun hours

"Peak sun hours" (PSH) is not daylight hours. It is the number of hours at a notional 1,000 W/m² that would deliver the same energy as the real day. A 100 W panel in 4 PSH makes at most about 400 Wh before losses.

Winter PSH is much lower than summer, and it falls the further south you go. Illustrative winter ranges for a flat panel might be around 2 in the far south and 4 to 5 in the tropical north or Red Centre, but check a proper source (such as NASA POWER or the Bureau of Meteorology) for your location and month. Tilting the panel towards the sun in winter helps, but a flat figure is the safer, more conservative one.

Step 3: Allow for losses

Real systems lose energy to heat, wiring, the solar controller, dust and imperfect angle. A common planning assumption is that you actually bank about 70% of the theoretical figure. That is an assumption, not a measurement. Dusty outback tracks or partial shade can push it lower.

Step 4: Calculate panel watts

Panel watts = daily Wh ÷ (PSH × 0.7)

Using 545 Wh:

| Winter PSH (illustrative) | Calculation | Panel size | Round up to | |---|---|---|---| | 5 | 545 ÷ (5 × 0.7 = 3.5) = 155.7 | 156 W | 160 W | | 4 | 545 ÷ 2.8 = 194.6 | 195 W | 200 W | | 3 | 545 ÷ 2.1 = 259.5 | 260 W | 260 W | | 2 | 545 ÷ 1.4 = 389.3 | 389 W | 400 W |

Round up to panel sizes you can actually buy and fit. Remember that a panel that is only just big enough leaves nothing for a run of cloudy days.

Step 5: Check the battery can bridge cloudy days

Solar sizing only works if the battery carries you through poor days. Choose how many days of autonomy you want. Two is a sensible starting point.

  • Energy needed: 545 Wh × 2 days = 1,090 Wh
  • Lithium (LiFePO4), assuming 80% usable and 12.8 V nominal: 1,090 ÷ 0.8 = 1,362 Wh, and 1,362 ÷ 12.8 = about 106 Ah. A 120 Ah battery fits.
  • AGM, assuming only 50% usable and 12 V nominal: 1,090 ÷ 0.5 = 2,180 Wh, and 2,180 ÷ 12 = about 182 Ah. A 200 Ah battery fits.

Usable depth of discharge varies by brand and chemistry, so follow your battery's datasheet.

Step 6: Add the driving top-up

If you move camp often, a DC-DC charger refills the battery while you drive and covers a lot of the winter gap. Its rating is the current it delivers on the 12V battery side, not what the alternator supplies. As a rough check, a 20 A charger running for 2 hours delivers about 40 Ah. At 12.8 V that is about 512 Wh, close to the whole 545 Wh daily load (assuming it holds near full output the whole time, which it won't always do). So driving a couple of hours a day makes a smaller panel workable, and a long stay in one spot makes a bigger panel worth it.

Common winter mistakes

  • Using summer sun figures. The darkest month of your trip is the one to plan for.
  • Using the fridge's peak watts as its daily use. This can overstate or understate it. Measure if you can.
  • Ignoring shade. A shadow across part of one panel can cut output sharply. Park the vehicle, not the panels, in the shade.
  • Forgetting losses. A 200 W panel does not make 200 W for four hours.
  • Skipping a margin. Add 10 to 20% if your load is uncertain.

Safety and limits

This article is general information, not electrical design. Cable sizes, fuses and breakers depend on your exact setup, and wiring faults can start fires. Have a licensed auto-electrician specify and check the installation, and use a licensed electrician for anything 240V or mains.
