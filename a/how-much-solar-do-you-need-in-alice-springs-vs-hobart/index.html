How Much Solar Do You Need in Alice Springs vs Hobart?

Short answer: the same 600 Wh/day camper needs roughly 190 W of solar in an Alice Springs winter, 280 W in Brisbane, 470 W in Melbourne and 570 W in Hobart, if you rely on solar alone. Where you travel changes the answer more than most people expect. These are illustrative figures, using the assumptions below.

Why location matters so much

Solar panel output depends on "peak sun hours": the daily sunshine energy, in kWh per square metre, treated as hours of full-strength sun. I pulled long-term monthly averages from NASA POWER (a flat, horizontal surface) for four cities. The darkest month, June, gives these figures:

| Place | June peak sun hours | December peak sun hours | Annual average | |---|---|---|---| | Alice Springs area | 4.24 | 7.42 | 6.25 | | Brisbane | 2.98 | 6.70 | 5.15 | | Melbourne | 1.79 | 7.05 | 4.43 | | Hobart | 1.46 | 6.67 | 4.04 |

Notice that summer is similar everywhere (about 6.7 to 7.4). Winter is where the south falls away: Hobart's June figure is about a third of Alice Springs'.

These are averages for a flat panel. Real days vary with cloud, dust, shade and panel tilt. Treat them as a planning start, not a promise.

The worked example

Assumptions (all illustrative, change them for your rig):

  • Daily use: 600 Wh/day (a typical-sized 12V fridge plus lights, phone charging and a water pump). Your own total will differ. Fridge use in particular varies a lot with the model, temperature and how often you open it.
  • System efficiency: 75%. This allows for panel heat, wiring, controller and battery losses. It is a rough planning figure, not a spec.
  • Planning sun hours: the darkest month's figure, rounded down to one decimal place.
  • Solar only. No driving, no DC-DC charging, no mains.

Formula: solar watts = daily Wh ÷ (sun hours × 0.75)

| Place | Planning sun hours (June) | Calculation | Solar needed | |---|---|---|---| | Alice Springs | 4.2 | 600 ÷ (4.2 × 0.75 = 3.15) | about 190 W | | Brisbane | 2.9 | 600 ÷ (2.9 × 0.75 = 2.175) | about 276 W | | Melbourne | 1.7 | 600 ÷ (1.7 × 0.75 = 1.275) | about 471 W | | Hobart | 1.4 | 600 ÷ (1.4 × 0.75 = 1.05) | about 571 W |

And for comparison, a December trip at the same load:

  • Alice Springs: 7.4 sun hours, so 600 ÷ (7.4 × 0.75 = 5.55) is about 108 W.
  • Melbourne: 7.0 sun hours, so 600 ÷ (7.0 × 0.75 = 5.25) is about 114 W.

So a 100 to 120 W panel can cover this load in summer almost anywhere, but only the Alice Springs-type winter can be covered with under 200 W.

What this means in practice

1. Size for the season you'll actually travel in. If you only camp from October to March, the summer row is the relevant one. If you head to Tasmania or Victoria in winter, solar alone becomes bulky and expensive, and a bigger battery plus driving time matters more.

2. Solar isn't your only source. If you drive most days, a DC-DC charger refills the battery while you travel. That can cover a good part of the daily load, so you may not need the full solar figure. The right split depends on how many hours you drive and on your alternator and vehicle wiring, which also limit what a DC-DC charger can actually deliver.

3. Add buffer days. Averages hide cloudy runs. Heavy cloud can cut output well below the average for several days in a row. A larger battery bridges those days; more solar helps on the good ones.

4. Tilt and shade. The NASA figures are for a flat surface. A panel roof-mounted flat and half shaded by a roof rack will do worse than the numbers. A panel you can angle towards the sun in winter may do better, though I haven't quantified that here.

5. Cold and lithium. Lithium (LiFePO4) batteries generally have limits on charging in near-freezing conditions, so check the maker's low-temperature charge rules if you camp in alpine areas or a cold southern winter. Some DC-DC chargers also need a minimum input voltage from the vehicle, so check the charger's specification against your vehicle.

A quick checklist

  • Add up your daily watt-hours honestly.
  • Pick the darkest month of the season you'll travel in, for your region.
  • Divide by sun hours × an efficiency figure (0.7 to 0.8 is a common range).
  • Subtract what driving and DC-DC will realistically supply.
  • Round up to the nearest panel size you can physically fit.
  • Have the wiring, fusing and installation done or checked by a licensed auto-electrician. Anything mains (240V) needs a licensed electrician.
