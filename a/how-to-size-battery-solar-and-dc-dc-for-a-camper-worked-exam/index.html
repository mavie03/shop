How to Size Battery, Solar and DC-DC for a Camper (Worked Example)

Sizing a 12V system by guesswork is how people end up with a flat battery on day three, or a pile of expensive gear they never needed. The good news: it comes down to five numbers, and you can get a sensible estimate with a pen and a phone calculator.

This article walks through all five, start to finish, using one example setup. Every figure in the example is an assumption for illustration. Swap in your own.

The five numbers

  • Daily watt-hours (Wh): how much energy you use in 24 hours.
  • Battery size: how much storage that needs, allowing for usable capacity.
  • Solar watts: how much panel it takes to refill that each day.
  • DC-DC charger size: how fast the alternator can top up while you drive.
  • Margin: a buffer for cloud, dust, heat and ageing.

Step 1: Add up daily watt-hours

For each device: watts × hours used per day = Wh. Check the label or manual for watts. For a compressor fridge, the motor only runs part of the time, so estimate a "duty cycle" (the share of the day it's actually running). It's higher in a hot Australian summer than a cool night in the Flinders.

Example: 4WD plus camper trailer, two people

| Device | Watts | Hours/day | Wh/day | |---|---|---|---| | 60L compressor fridge | 45 | 24 × 40% duty = 9.6 | about 432 | | LED lights | 10 | 4 | 40 | | Water pump | 60 | 0.2 | 12 | | Phones, camera, laptop charging | – | – | 100 | | Total | | | about 584 Wh |

The fridge is usually the biggest load, so measure it if you can. A cheap plug-in 12V power meter over a day gives a far better figure than a brochure.

Step 2: Add a margin

Real days are messier than a spreadsheet. A 20% margin is a reasonable starting point (an assumption you can adjust):

584 × 1.2 = about 700 Wh per day

If you want to run for more than a day without sun or driving, multiply by the number of days of "autonomy" you want. Two days would be about 1,400 Wh.

Step 3: Convert to battery size

Batteries can't be drained fully without shortening their life. The usable share depends on type. These are common rules of thumb; check your battery's datasheet:

  • Lithium (LiFePO4): often about 80–90% usable.
  • AGM: commonly planned at around 50% usable.

Lithium, one day of storage: 700 Wh ÷ 0.9 usable = about 778 Wh. At about 12.8V, that's 778 ÷ 12.8 = about 61 Ah. In practice you'd buy a 100Ah battery, which leaves room for growth and a cloudy second day.

AGM, one day of storage: 700 Wh ÷ 0.5 = 1,400 Wh. At 12V, that's about 117 Ah, so a 120Ah or 135Ah battery, and it's much heavier. This weight difference is why many people pay more for lithium.

Step 4: Size the solar

You need to know your "peak sun hours": roughly, how many hours a day the sun is at full strength equivalent. This varies a lot across Australia and by season. NASA's POWER project publishes long-term averages by location. Use the darkest month you'll travel in, not the best, so winter doesn't catch you out.

Panels also lose output to heat, dust, wiring and the charge controller. A 25% loss allowance is a cautious assumption.

Formula: daily Wh ÷ (sun hours × 0.75) = panel watts

Assuming 4 peak sun hours (a plausible figure for a southern winter, but check your location):

700 ÷ (4 × 0.75) = about 233 W, so a 250W panel or two 130–150W panels.

In central Australia in winter, or the tropical north in the dry, you'll often get more sun than that, so you could get away with less. In Tasmania or Victoria in July, you may get less, and you'd need more panel or more driving. Shade from the camper, a flat-mounted panel and a dusty dirt road all reduce the real figure, which is why a margin matters.

Step 5: Size the DC-DC charger

A DC-DC charger sits between your starter battery and your auxiliary battery. It charges the second battery properly from the alternator, and it's especially important with modern "smart" alternators that drop voltage. Many lithium batteries also need one.

A common rule of thumb is to size the charger at roughly 20–30% of battery capacity in amps for lithium. For a 100Ah battery, that's 20–30A. A 25A unit is a typical choice. Larger isn't always better: the charger also has to suit your alternator, wiring and the battery's maximum charge rate. Always check the battery maker's specs.

Driving charge helps a lot: an hour of driving at 25A is up to about 25Ah (roughly 300 Wh) in ideal conditions. Real figures are lower once you allow for heat and voltage drop.

Common mistakes

  • Using the fridge's label watts as if it ran all day. Duty cycle matters, but so does heat. Park it in shade and give it ventilation.
  • Sizing solar on a summer sun figure. Plan for your darkest travel month.
  • Ignoring usable capacity. A "100Ah" AGM isn't 100Ah you can use.
  • Buying the charger or panel first. Do the load total first, then work outward.
  • Forgetting the inverter. If you add one, its idle draw and conversion losses add to your total.

What you should leave to a professional

This article covers sizing only. It does not cover cable thickness, fuse or breaker ratings, or how to wire anything. Those depend on current, cable length and your vehicle, and mistakes can cause fires. Get a licensed auto-electrician for 12V installation work, and a licensed electrician for anything 240V or mains.

Shopping tip

Once you have your numbers, compare the same specs across several retailers, and note battery type, warranty and the DC-DC charger's maximum input and output. Prices vary a lot, so keep a simple list rather than relying on memory.
