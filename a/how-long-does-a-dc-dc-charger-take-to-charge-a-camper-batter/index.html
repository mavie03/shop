How Long Does a DC-DC Charger Take to Charge a Camper Battery?

Short answer: on a 100Ah lithium battery that's 20% full, a 20A DC-DC charger takes roughly 5 hours of driving, a 25A about 4, a 40A about 2.5 and a 50A about 2. Those figures assume the charger delivers about 80% of its rating on average. That 80% is my assumption, not a measured figure. Your result will differ.

Quick table (100Ah battery, 20% to 100%)

You need to put back 80Ah. The "real output" column is 80% of the charger's rating (assumption).

| Charger rating | Real output (assumed) | Time for 80Ah | Best case (100% of rating) | |---|---|---|---| | 20A | 16A | 5.0 hours | 4.0 hours | | 25A | 20A | 4.0 hours | 3.2 hours | | 40A | 32A | 2.5 hours | 2.0 hours | | 50A | 40A | 2.0 hours | 1.6 hours |

The maths is simple: amp hours needed ÷ amps delivered = hours. For the 25A: 80 ÷ 20 = 4.0 hours.

Why you won't get the full rated amps

  • Heat. Many chargers reduce output when they get hot, and engine bays and cargo areas get hot in an Australian summer.
  • Voltage drop. Long or thin cable runs between the vehicle and the battery reduce what arrives.
  • Taper. Near full, charging current falls away. This is most obvious with AGM and lead-acid batteries, where the last 20% can take much longer than the first 80%.
  • Alternator behaviour. Some modern vehicles with smart alternators vary their voltage, which can limit the charger's input.

Lithium (LiFePO4) accepts high current for most of the charge, so the table is a fair guide. For AGM, treat the times as a minimum and expect the final top-up to be slow.

How much current does the charger pull from the vehicle?

This is the part guides often skip, and it's easy to get muddled. A DC-DC charger's rating (say 25A) is the current going into your battery. The current drawn from the vehicle side is different, because of charger efficiency and voltage.

Illustrative assumptions: the battery side runs at 14.4V, the charger is 90% efficient, and the vehicle side sits at about 13.5V.

| Charger | Output power (rating × 14.4V) | Input power (÷ 0.9) | Input current (÷ 13.5V) | |---|---|---|---| | 20A | 288W | 320W | about 24A | | 25A | 360W | 400W | about 30A | | 40A | 576W | 640W | about 47A | | 50A | 720W | 800W | about 59A |

So a 50A charger can draw close to 60A from the vehicle at full output. That's a real load on the alternator and wiring, on top of what the vehicle already needs. Check your alternator's capacity, and get cable size and fuse rating from a licensed auto-electrician. Those depend on your vehicle and cable length, and I'm not giving them here.

Worked example: a weekend camper trailer

Assumptions (all illustrative):

  • A 12V fridge and a few lights use about 50Ah per day (about 600Wh at 12V).
  • A 100Ah lithium battery.
  • You drive about 2 hours on travel days.

With a 25A charger (20A real): 20A × 2 hours = 40Ah put back. That nearly covers a 50Ah day, leaving you about 10Ah short per day of driving. Add solar and you're fine for a few days.

With a 40A charger (32A real): 32A × 2 hours = 64Ah. That covers the day with margin, as long as your vehicle's alternator and wiring can support it.

With a 20A charger (16A real): 16A × 2 hours = 32Ah. That covers about two-thirds of the day, so you'd rely on solar for the rest.

What does this mean for choosing a size?

  • Short drives, camping for several days in one spot: driving alone won't refill the battery. Solar matters more than a bigger charger.
  • Long travel days (big lappers): a 20A or 25A charger can do the job, because the hours are there.
  • Larger batteries (200Ah and up) or short hops between camps: a 40A or 50A charger gets more amp hours in per hour of driving, if the vehicle can supply it.

Sources differ on sizing rules of thumb. Some retailers suggest a charger of about 10-20% of battery capacity, others 20-30%. For a 100Ah battery that's anywhere from 10A to 30A. Working from your own daily use and drive time, as above, is more reliable than a percentage.

Quick check you can do yourself

  • Estimate daily use in Ah (fridge, lights, pump, charging devices).
  • Estimate your usual daily driving hours.
  • Multiply your candidate charger's rating by 0.8, then by the drive hours.
  • If the result is below your daily use, you need more driving, more solar or a bigger charger.

Safety note

Wiring a DC-DC charger involves high currents in a vehicle. Cable sizes, fuses and mounting should be specified and installed by a licensed auto-electrician. Anything involving 240V mains needs a licensed electrician. This article is general information, not electrical design advice.
