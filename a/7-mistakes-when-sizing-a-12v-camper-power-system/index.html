7 Mistakes When Sizing a 12V Camper Power System

Short answer: most oversized, undersized or wasted 12V setups come from the same seven slips: guessing the fridge's use, ignoring the darkest month, mixing up Ah and Wh, forgetting losses, buying solar without a plan for cloudy days, relying on one charging source, and treating wiring as an afterthought. Fix those and the sizing itself is simple arithmetic.

All figures below are illustrative assumptions, not measurements of your gear. Swap in your own numbers.

The worked example we'll use

A weekend-to-week tourer with a 4WD and camper trailer:

| Item | Assumption | Wh per day | |---|---|---| | 12V fridge | about 480 Wh/day in warm weather (check your model's data) | 480 | | LED lights | 10 W x 4 h | 40 | | Phones, camera, laptop | allowance | 80 | | Water pump | 40 W x 0.25 h | 10 | | Total | | 610 |

Check: 480 + 40 + 80 + 10 = 610 Wh/day.

Mistake 1: Guessing the fridge

The fridge is usually the biggest load, and the label wattage is not its daily use. A fridge cycles on and off, and it works harder in a hot car or trailer. Use the manufacturer's daily figure if they give one, or measure it with a DC meter over 24 hours. Quoted daily figures vary widely between fridges and conditions, so a measured number beats any rule of thumb.

Mistake 2: Sizing for a sunny day, not the worst month

Solar sized for January in Darwin won't cope with July in Tasmania. Size for the darkest month you'll actually camp in. Sun hours also differ a lot across the country: Alice Springs and Hobart are far apart in winter. Long-term averages for a flat panel are a starting point, and real days vary with cloud, dust, shade and tilt.

Mistake 3: Mixing up Ah and Wh

Batteries are sold in amp hours, appliances are often listed in watts. Convert with Wh = Ah x volts. On a nominal 12V basis:

  • 610 Wh / 12 V = 50.8 Ah per day.

If you don't convert, a "100Ah" battery sounds like a lot until you realise it's roughly 1,200 Wh (100 x 12), and you can't use all of it.

Mistake 4: Forgetting usable capacity and spare days

Decide how many days you want to run with no charging, then allow for the portion of the battery you can safely use. Assuming 2 days and 80% usable (an assumption; check your battery's specification):

  • 50.8 Ah x 2 days = 101.7 Ah
  • 101.7 / 0.8 = about 127 Ah, so a 150Ah battery is the next sensible size up.

AGM batteries are normally discharged less deeply than lithium, so they need a bigger rated capacity for the same usable energy.

Mistake 5: Solar sized with no loss allowance

Panels rarely deliver their rated watts. A common rule of thumb is panel watts = daily Wh / (sun hours x 0.7), where 0.7 allows for losses. With our 610 Wh:

| Winter sun hours (assumed) | Calculation | Solar needed | |---|---|---| | 4 h | 610 / (4 x 0.7) = 610 / 2.8 | about 218 W | | 3 h | 610 / (3 x 0.7) = 610 / 2.1 | about 290 W | | 5 h | 610 / (5 x 0.7) = 610 / 3.5 | about 174 W |

The spread is the point: the same trip needs roughly 175 W to 290 W depending on the sun you assume. Round up to the panel sizes that actually fit your roof or blanket, and remember a solar controller must be matched to the panels. Check the controller's specifications or ask the retailer.

Mistake 6: Relying on one charging source

Solar alone struggles under trees and in a run of cloudy days. A DC-DC charger tops up from the vehicle while you drive. As a rough 12V-side estimate, a 20A charger delivers up to 20 A x 12 V = 240 Wh per driving hour, so two hours gives about 480 Wh. That is a ceiling: real output depends on the alternator, the vehicle's wiring and battery temperature. If you run both solar and DC-DC, check their combined current against your battery's maximum charge rating, which is set by the battery and its BMS (battery management system).

Mistake 7: Treating wiring as an afterthought

Cable size and fuse rating depend on current, length and installation, and published guides don't all agree. Note that a DC-DC charger draws more current on its input side than it delivers, so don't size cables from the output figure. This article deliberately gives no cable or fuse sizes. Have a licensed auto-electrician design and check this part, and use a licensed electrician for anything 240V or mains.

A quick pre-purchase checklist

  • Measured or manufacturer fridge daily use (Wh).
  • Every other load, with watts and hours.
  • Darkest month and region you'll camp in.
  • Days of autonomy you want.
  • Battery chemistry and usable percentage.
  • Solar watts with losses included, and where they'll physically fit.
  • DC-DC size and the battery's charge-current limit.
  • Quotes from an auto-electrician for the install.
