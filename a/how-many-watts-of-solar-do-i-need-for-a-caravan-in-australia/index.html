How Many Watts of Solar Do I Need for a Caravan in Australia?

Short answer: most weekend caravanners are fine with about 100–200W of solar, regular tourers with 200–300W, and full-time or big-lap travellers with 300–500W or more. Your real number depends on how many watt-hours you use per day and how much sun you get in the season you travel. The maths is simple, and you can do it with a calculator.

The formula

Solar watts = daily watt-hours ÷ peak sun hours × 1.25

  • Daily watt-hours (Wh): everything you run, each device's watts × hours used per day, added up.
  • Peak sun hours: the number of hours per day of full-strength sun (1,000 W/m²) your panel effectively gets. It is much lower than daylight hours.
  • × 1.25: a 25% buffer for heat, dust, cabling losses, imperfect angle and charging losses.

Quick table (illustrative assumptions)

Peak sun hours below are illustrative round numbers, not figures for a specific town: 5.5 for a good summer day, 4 for a typical shoulder or mild winter day, and 2.5 for a poor, cloudy or southern-winter day. Real values vary by location, tilt and shade.

| Travel style | Daily use | 5.5 sun hrs | 4 sun hrs | 2.5 sun hrs | |---|---|---|---|---| | Weekender | 450 Wh | about 102W | about 141W | 225W | | Regular tourer | 800 Wh | about 182W | 250W | 400W | | Full-timer / big lapper | 1,500 Wh | about 341W | about 469W | 750W |

Notice the spread. The same caravan needs more than double the panel in a poor-sun winter than on a good summer day. That is why many guides suggest sizing for the season you actually travel in, not the best one.

Worked example: a regular tourer (800 Wh a day)

Assumptions (all estimates, swap in your own):

| Item | Calculation | Wh/day | |---|---|---| | 12V compressor fridge/freezer | average use over 24 hours | 500 | | LED lights | 20W total × 4 hours | 80 | | Water pump | 40W × 0.25 hour | 10 | | Phones and laptop | 60W × 1 hour | 60 | | TV or other gear | 30W × 5 hours | 150 | | Total | | 800 |

Solar, at 4 peak sun hours: 800 ÷ 4 = 200W, × 1.25 = 250W. Solar, at 5.5 hours: 800 ÷ 5.5 = 145.5W, × 1.25 = about 182W. Solar, at 2.5 hours: 800 ÷ 2.5 = 320W, × 1.25 = 400W.

If you travel north in winter, 250W may suit you well. If you stay in the south in winter, either carry more panel or accept that you'll top up from the vehicle.

Don't forget the battery

Solar only fills a battery. It doesn't cover the night. A simple battery check:

Battery Wh = daily Wh × days of autonomy ÷ usable fraction. Then divide by 12 for approximate amp-hours.

For 800 Wh a day, two days without charging, and lithium (assume 80% usable): 800 × 2 = 1,600 ÷ 0.8 = 2,000 Wh. 2,000 ÷ 12 = about 167Ah, so a 150–200Ah battery is the sensible range. (Using 12.8V nominal for lithium would give about 156Ah. Both land in the same size class.)

For the other two tiers, the same method gives about 94Ah (a 100Ah battery) for 450 Wh a day, and about 313Ah for 1,500 Wh a day. Lead-acid and AGM batteries usually have a lower usable fraction, so you'd need a bigger battery for the same energy.

Can solar on the roof do it all?

Often, yes, if you camp in the sun for several days. But a roof that's shaded, a van parked under trees, or a run of cloudy days will leave you short. This is where a DC-DC charger from the tow vehicle helps. It charges the caravan battery while you drive, and it's also useful for travellers who move camp daily. As a rough rule, many retailers suggest sizing a DC-DC charger at 10–30% of battery Ah (sources disagree on the exact range), but that is a separate calculation from solar.

Common mistakes

  • Using the best summer sun for winter trips. The panel that's fine in January may be marginal in July.
  • Forgetting the fridge is most of the load. It's usually the biggest daily number. Measure it if you can.
  • Counting panel watts as if they were delivered watts. That's what the 25% buffer is for.
  • Flat-mounted panels in winter. Tilting portable panels toward the sun can help, but fixed roof panels can't be tilted.
  • Skipping the regulator. Panels connect to the battery via a solar regulator (MPPT or PWM), and its rating must match your panels and battery.
  • Undersizing the battery and oversizing the panel. Both need to match each other.

Safety note

This article is general information, not electrical design. Cable sizes, fuses, breakers and the wiring of panels, regulators and chargers affect fire risk and warranties, so have a licensed auto-electrician do or check that work. Anything involving 240V or mains power needs a licensed electrician.
