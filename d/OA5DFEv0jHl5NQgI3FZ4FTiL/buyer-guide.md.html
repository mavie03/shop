Size Your 12V Power System: A Plain Guide for Australian Caravans, Camper Trailers and 4WDs

General information only. Not electrical design or installation advice. Have your final design and install checked by a licensed auto-electrician. Anything 240V or mains needs a licensed electrician. All figures are estimates. Prices are not quotes.

This guide comes with a calculator (power-budget-calculator.html). Open it in any browser. It works offline.

---

1. The five numbers you need

  • Daily energy use (Wh). What your gear uses in 24 hours.
  • Battery size (Ah). How much you can store.
  • Solar (W). How much you can put back on a bad-ish day.
  • DC-DC charger (A). How fast the tow vehicle tops up the battery.
  • A parts list. So you can shop around.

2. Step 1: list your loads

For each device: watts x quantity x hours per day = Wh per day.

  • Check the label or manual for watts.
  • A compressor fridge doesn't run all day. It cycles. Use the maker's Wh/day figure if there is one, and add a margin for hot weather. In the Australian outback the fridge is usually the biggest load.
  • Add phones, lights, pump, fans, laptop, router or Starlink.
  • Add a 15% margin for losses and guesswork. The calculator does this.

3. Step 2: battery

Battery Ah = daily Wh x days of autonomy / (usable fraction x battery volts).

  • Lithium (LiFePO4): about 80% usable, 12.8 V.
  • AGM/lead-acid: about 50% usable, so roughly double the Ah for the same result.
  • Autonomy is how many days you want to run without sun or driving. 1.5 days is a sensible start. Stay somewhere shady for longer? Use 2-3.

Take the next size up that you can buy.

4. Step 3: solar

Solar W = daily Wh / (peak sun hours x system efficiency).

  • Peak sun hours are the sun-equivalent hours per day. Use a winter-low figure for where you'll travel, so the system works in your worst month, not your best.
  • Allow about 75% efficiency for heat, dust, cables, shade and a flat panel.
  • About the calculator's regional figures: they are long-term NASA POWER averages (satellite-derived, all-sky, flat panel) for the darkest month of each season, rounded down a little. For Alice Springs the figure also agrees with Bureau of Meteorology data. Real days vary with cloud, dust, shade and panel tilt, so leave margin. In the tropical wet season, storm runs are far darker than the average, so add autonomy days.
  • To look up another place: go to power.larc.nasa.gov and read the long-term monthly solar value in kWh/m2/day for your darkest month. That equals peak sun hours. If you only have a BoM figure in MJ/m2 per day, divide by 3.6. Type the result into the calculator's custom box.

5. Step 4: DC-DC charger

  • Most modern tow vehicles have smart or variable-voltage alternators. A plain battery link doesn't charge properly from them. A DC-DC charger fixes that.
  • A starting size is 0.2-0.5 x battery Ah in amps. 0.3 is typical. Choose the next standard size at or above that. Check your alternator's rating and the charger's specs.
  • A DC-DC with built-in MPPT can replace a separate solar regulator.

6. Step 5: cables, fuses, wiring: take it to a professional

This guide gives no cable sizes or fuse ratings. They depend on run length, mounting and equipment, and a wrong choice can cause a fire. Ask your licensed auto-electrician. Fuse or breaker near each source and the battery.

8. Australian conditions

  • Heat: fridges use more power. Keep batteries ventilated and out of direct sun.
  • Cold: lithium shouldn't be charged below freezing. Check your battery's spec.
  • Dust and corrugations: secure mounts, sealed connections.
  • Shade: add panel capacity or use a portable panel you can move.
  • Sun figures: cloud and dust reduce output. Leave margin.

9. Common mistakes

  • Using the fridge's label watts instead of average daily use.
  • Sizing solar for summer, then camping in July.
  • Buying a bigger battery without a charger to fill it.
  • Skipping the DC-DC on a smart-alternator vehicle.
  • Guessing cable and fuse sizes.
